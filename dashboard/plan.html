read this first
This problem is two separate losses, not one. Loss B is real and we can act on it. Loss A is larger, but it may be a fault in the simulation rather than in the robot, and no amount of training can fix that. Read Loss B first.

In a walk, a planted foot is supposed to stay put while the body moves over it. Gray's planted feet do not. Four things were measured on the same walk: what the gait commands the leg to do, what the leg actually does, how fast the planted foot skids on the floor, and how fast the body ends up going. Between the first and the last, roughly four fifths of the commanded stride disappears — and it disappears in two quite different ways, which have to be separated before anything is spent on either.

These are four independent measurements of one walk, not four lines of a balance sheet. They do not subtract neatly into one another, and this page does not pretend they do: no figure below is obtained by taking one of them away from another. In the chart above, the dashed outline on the second bar is Loss A and the third bar is Loss B; they are measured separately and are not two halves of one total.

Loss B — the one we can act on 68mm/s measured at the foot, against the ground

The planted foot skids along the floor

A planted foot is meant to be a fixed point that the body pulls itself over. Gray's slides backwards across the floor at 68 mm/s while it is carrying load. This one is real whatever the servo model turns out to be: it is measured from the foot's movement against the ground, not inferred from a torque, so a stiffer servo would not make it go away. It is also the only one of the two that the proposed stance-anchoring reward actually targets. Stage B is a test of Loss B and of nothing else.

Loss A — the larger one 104mm/s 39% of what was commanded

The leg never achieves what it is told

The gait asks each planted leg for 267 mm/s of backward sweep and gets 163. Nothing is slipping in this one — the leg simply does not get there. In simulation the hip-pitch servo sits at its torque limit 72.6% of the time and finishes 25° away from the angle it was commanded. That is the signature of a servo being asked for more than it has.

may not be real
Loss A may be an artefact of the simulation rather than a fault in the robot. The servo model is the single largest unquantified risk in the whole project (finding B13): it models each DS3218MG as 20 N·m/rad, which works out as a proportional band (how far the servo has to be pushed off its commanded angle before it is giving all the torque it has) of only 5.6° — a distinctly soft servo. A modern digital servo is likely to be a good deal stiffer than that. If it is, then much of Loss A does not exist on the real machine at all, and the simulated robot has been learning to compensate for a sag that is not there.

It cannot be checked either way today, because the robot is disassembled and there is no bench measurement to check it against.

So do not chase this one first. No reward and no amount of training can fix a wrong servo model; only a servo, a bench and a torque measurement at reassembly can. Until then the plan merely contains it: servo stiffness randomisation (deliberately varying the stiffness between simulated robots) widens from 0.7–1.4× to 0.5–4.0×, which costs nothing and forces the learned behaviour to work across every plausible servo. That contains the risk. It does not resolve it.

Loss B in detail — what it is not

It is not a grip problem

There is 16 N of traction available against a 0.1 N demand — a margin of more than a hundred times. The feet are not sliding because they cannot hold. They are sliding because four open-loop legs (commanded blind, with no feedback of any kind) are each running their own fixed pattern with no knowledge of what the other three are doing, so they pull against each other and something has to give.

It is not a speed limit

The same skid shows up at every stride length from 60 to 160 mm and every gait period from 0.6 to 1.2 s. It does not go away when you slow down or shorten the step, which makes it structural — built into how the gait is generated, not a symptom of pushing the servos too hard.

What fixing it buys, stated honestly

Loss B is 68 mm/s of foot movement going into scrubbing the floor rather than into travel, on the same servos, the same stride length and the same landing impact — no extra load on any part. Recovering it is worth real speed. It is deliberately not quoted as an "after" figure: the four measurements above do not subtract into one another, so any single resulting speed would be invented. Stage B measures slip directly, before and after, and that measurement is what decides it. Loss A is not in this number, and no reward will fix Loss A.

The back feet scrape

The walk asks every foot to lift 34 mm. The back feet manage 0.8 mm.

How high each foot lifts
Asked for 34.0 mm. Front feet reach 21.6 mm, back feet 0.8 mm.

Why it matters. On carpet, or a door strip, a foot that low catches and the robot trips.

Show me the evidence

Listed as B2 in the full set of thirteen findings.

The back feet clear the floor by 0.8 mm — they are shuffled forward, not stepped. On a flat hard floor it gets away with it. On carpet, or a threshold strip, it trips. This is also why the front legs look like they are doing all the work: they are.

It is measured over a full walk cycle, as the highest point each foot reaches. The front feet reach 21.6 mm of the 34.0 mm commanded, which is already short; the back feet reach 2.4% of it.

connected
This is the direct cause of finding B3 below: the rear legs carry 67% of the weight but supply only 28% of the push. A foot that never lifts cannot be placed anywhere useful, so it cannot push. The two are one fault, seen from two ends.
how it is fixed

Stage C, together with the hips. Two parts: pay the robot for lifting a swinging foot clear of the ground, and keep the body level front-to-back — levelling the body raises the back feet without asking any servo for more travel than it already has.

Why it matters. The robot cannot turn and cannot step sideways, because of one number in one file.

Show me the evidence

Listed as B4 in the full set of thirteen findings.

cause
gray/gait.py builds every foot target as neutral + [forward, 0.0, up]. That hardcoded 0.0 is the sideways component — it is written in as zero and never computed. It is the only reason the robot cannot turn or strafe. The legs can already reach a 140 mm sideways stride, so the hardware is not the limit. One number in one file is.
what the fix actually is

For a commanded body movement (forward, sideways, turn), each foot must travel through its stance along −(v + ω × r). That one expression automatically gives every leg its own stride length and its own direction, which is what turning is. Turning, sideways walking and variable ride height are therefore not three features — they are the same change, made once.

measured travel, against what each servo has

Thigh joints use 44–55° of 270°. Knee joints use 24–28°. Hip joints use 0.0° — not a small number, exactly zero, in every sample of every run.

04

The AI is fighting its own safety limit

28%
how often its correction is stopped by the safety limit

The AI may bend each joint by up to 11 deg, for safety. It now asks for more than that 28% of the time.

How often the safety limit has to stop it
Measured at three points during one training run. It is going the wrong way.

Why it matters. The safe fallback only holds while the corrections stay small, and they no longer are.

Show me the evidence

Listed as B10 in the full set of thirteen findings.

Words used here: the policy — the AI, the learned layer that adds a small correction on top of the walk we wrote by hand. clipped — cut down to a limit: a correction of 0.31 rad against a ±0.2 rad ceiling is clipped to 0.2, and the trainer is never told that 0.11 of what it asked for was thrown away.

While training, the robot deliberately adds a random wobble to its corrections so it can discover things — and that wobble is supposed to shrink as it settles on an answer. It is doing the opposite: 0.101 at the start, then 0.191 at round 300, 0.286 at round 700, 0.313 at round 1150, never turning over. The safety argument for this whole design is that a bad policy falls back toward a gait that already walks — and that only holds while the correction stays small. At round 1100 the policy is fighting the safety limit rather than nudging the gait.

It also feeds itself: a correction that gets clipped teaches nothing, so nothing pushes the wobble back down, while a separate bonus for "keep your options open" keeps paying for it to widen.

how it is fixed

Stage A, three parts, none of which costs any graphics-card time. Start logging the share of corrections hitting the limit — today it is not recorded anywhere, which is why it ran this far unnoticed. Add a small penalty on the size of the correction before the limit clips it, so there is finally something pushing back. Fade the "keep your options open" bonus from 0.005 to 0.0005 over the first third of a run.

the plan

Four stages, in this order

Cheapest and most certain first. There is a hard stop between the first and the second.

Stage A

Fix the measuring first

what we do
Repair how we measure, before spending any card time.
what it costs
0 hours of card time. About 2 hours of my time.
what you get
Numbers you can trust when we ask "did that help?"
Show me the detail

Nothing here trains anything. It is entirely about making sure that when we later ask "did that help?", the answer we get back is true. We have already lost a full run to a scoring bug, so this comes first. Fixes B6, B7, B8, B10, B12.

  • A1 — rebuild the scoring harness (the separate program that scores a saved copy after the fact, and decides which one is best). Five random starts, five commanded speeds spanning −40 to +80 mm/s including zero (where a robot that has learned to freeze can hide), and eight randomisation draws per attempt. Fixes B8.
  • A2 — re-measure the classical gait properly. Thirty random starts × five commands, quoted as a range rather than a single number, and replace that single number everywhere it appears. Fixes B7.
  • A3 — stop the noise runaway. Start logging the share of corrections hitting the safety limit (currently invisible); add a small penalty, at weight −0.002, on the size of the correction before the safety limit clips it; and fade the "keep your options open" bonus from 0.005 to 0.0005 over the first third of a run. Fixes B10.
  • A4 — delete two dead scoring items. One accounts for 0.05% of the total score and points the wrong way; the other cannot ever fire, because a different limit always acts first.
  • A5 — fix the straightness reward. Widen 0.050 → 0.150, halve its weight 1.0 → 0.5, and add a term that measures how far sideways of the intended line the robot ended up. That measure is immune to footfall ripple by construction rather than by tuning. Fixes B12.
  • A6 — only count a foot as down if it is carrying more than 1 N. Stops us paying for bounce. Fixes B6.
  • A7 — housekeeping for a long run. Disk retention (currently unmanaged: a month writes about 160 GB of checkpoints and video, and the machine has 78 GB free), an automatic restart from the newest saved copy after a crash, alarms, and a graphics-card power cap at 85% — on this card the last 15% buys 3–5% throughput and roughly doubles fan duty over 700 hours.
what could go wrong

The new harness is slower to run than the old one, because it does 200 times as much work per saved copy. That is the price of an answer you can rely on. Nothing in Stage A can make the robot worse; the worst case is that it costs two hours and tells us the old numbers were fine.

Stage B

Stop the feet sliding

what we do
Take points away from any loaded foot that slides.
what it costs
8 hours of card time. None of my time.
what you get
A straight answer on whether the 68 mm/s skid can be trained away.
Show me the detail

Add one scoring item: any foot that is carrying load gets penalised for moving sideways across the floor, at weight −8.0 — a large weight, because the skid is the largest loss we are confident is real. Then log the measured slip directly against the 68 mm/s baseline, so we can see whether it actually fell. Addresses Loss B of B1, and nothing else.

what it buys

The target is the 68 mm/s the planted foot currently spends scrubbing the floor. Recovering that is worth real speed, on the same servos, the same stride and the same landing impact. No resulting speed is quoted here on purpose: the four measurements in B1 are independent and do not subtract into one another, so the only honest answer comes from measuring slip before and after — which is exactly what this stage does. Eight hours is a cheap test of the most valuable hypothesis we have.

what it explicitly does not buy

Loss A — the 104 mm/s the leg is told to sweep and does not — is untouched by this stage and by every other stage in the plan. A reward cannot make a servo stronger, and it certainly cannot correct a servo model that may be wrong. Loss A is closed by a bench measurement at reassembly (B13), not here.

what could go wrong

Weighted too hard, the robot finds the lazy way out: it shortens its stride until there is barely any foot movement to penalise. That would score well and walk worse. This is exactly why the plan logs slip as a measured quantity in its own right rather than inferring success from the score. If stride length collapses, we will see it immediately and back the weight off.

Stage C

Unlock the hips

what we do
Fix the one number that keeps the hip servos at zero.
what it costs
12 hours of card time. About 1 day of my time.
what you get
Turning, sideways steps, and back feet that lift.
Show me the detail

One edit to gray/gait.py delivers turning, sideways walking and variable ride height together. These are not three features — they are the same change. For a commanded body movement (forward, sideways, turn), each foot must travel through its stance along −(v + ω × r), which automatically gives every leg its own stride length and its own direction.   • Also in this stage: fix the back feet that only clear 0.8 mm (B2), and add a scoring item that pays for lifting a swinging foot clear of the ground.
flagged
gray/gait.py is imported unchanged by the Raspberry Pi on the robot and may only use numpy and pyyaml. This edit is pure trigonometry — no new imports. The old way of calling it keeps working, and a test proves that a forward-only command reproduces today's walk bit for bit.
what could go wrong

Turning needs its own scoring item to stop the robot freezing, or it recreates the failure that already cost us a full run. The existing "distance covered" reward returns exactly zero when no forward speed is commanded — so for a pure turn-on-the-spot command, every remaining scoring item is maximised by standing perfectly still. Adding turning without adding a reason to turn would teach it to do nothing, very smoothly.

Stage D

The long runs

what we do
Try 48 settings, keep the best 8, then train for real.
what it costs
200 hours of card time. None of my time.
what you get
The finished walk, judged on its worst run, not its best.
Show me the detail

Settings first, then the real runs. A sweep of 48 configurations × 800 rounds (about 24 hours), then the best 8 of those repeated with 3 different random starts each (another 24 hours). That is 7% of the month spent locking down the settings used for the other 93% — and it is the difference between one long run that happens to work and knowing why it worked.

the most promising single setting to test

The trainer currently learns from 0.48 s chunks of experience. That is shorter than one 0.6 s gait cycle, and shorter than the 0.6 s smoothing its own reward uses. Every learning step is therefore looking at less than a full stride — it is being asked to judge a walk without ever seeing a complete step.

how a winner is chosen

Real runs at full randomisation with 5 random starts. A configuration counts as better only if it wins on its worst seed, not its average. This is going onto brittle printed parts — the bad day is the one that matters.

what could go wrong

This is where the time is, and it is only worth spending if Stage A passed its gate. If the harness is wrong, 200 hours of selection is 200 hours of coin-flipping. That is precisely the failure this ordering is designed to prevent.

Stop — hard stop

Stage A only passes if the new measuring method gets two known answers right

We already know these two answers, from careful measurement by hand. The new method has to find both on its own, without being told:

  1. The older saved copy walks better than the walk we wrote by hand, at all five speeds.
  2. The newer saved copy is worse than the older one.

If it cannot re-judge two answers we already know, it cannot pick the best from thousands.

If it fails, we spend 0 hours of card time and fix the measuring method instead.

Show me the evidence

The two known answers are: checkpoint 950 beats the classical gait at all five commanded speeds, and checkpoint 1100 is worse than checkpoint 950. Both were established by hand, outside the harness. A checkpoint is a saved copy of the learned behaviour at one training round.

This is the only honest test available. If the harness cannot separate three checkpoints whose answer we already know, it certainly cannot pick the best out of the thousands a long run produces — and every hour of Stage B, C and D would be spent selecting at random while believing otherwise. It is a stop-work condition, not a preference.

the scoring

What changes in the scoring

The robot gets points for doing things right and loses points for doing them wrong. We are adding ten new rules, deleting two, and changing four.

The ten new rules

  • loseLose points for sliding a foot along the ground.
  • loseLose points for dragging a foot that should be swinging forward.
  • loseLose points for landing far more times than the walk asked for.
  • loseLose points for pushing a servo as hard as it will go.
  • loseLose points for letting the body tip nose-up or nose-down.
  • loseLose a lot of points, once, for falling over.
  • loseLose points for asking for a big correction, even if we cut it down.
  • loseLose a huge number of points for letting the body sink too low.
  • gainGain points for keeping three feet properly on the ground.
  • gainGain points for ending up on the line it was aiming for.

The two we delete

  • dropGo easy on the servos. It is 0.05% of the score and points the wrong way.
  • dropStay off the joint end stops. Another limit always acts first, so it never fires.

The four we change

  • changeThe prize for covering ground goes up by half.
  • changeThe penalty for hard landings goes up 200 times. It was switched off before.
  • changeThe "walk straight" rule is halved and loosened, so normal rocking is not punished.
  • changeThe "move joints smoothly" rule is trimmed a little to make room.

The bigger prize is not optional. It is what stops the robot deciding that standing still pays better than walking.

Show me the numbers

Sixteen items change. A seventeenth is listed and explicitly deferred, so that a fault we have diagnosed but chosen not to act on yet is visible rather than absent. The scoring items are the only instruction the robot ever receives — anything not scored does not exist to it.

the ceiling

What this robot will never do

None of these is fixable by training. Approve the plan knowing where the ceiling is.

  • ✕No stairs. The riser is taller than its hips.
  • ✕No feedback from any joint, ever. The servos cannot report where they ended up.
  • ✕No sense of place. It never knows where it is, or which way it points.
  • ✕No sight. The camera and lidar in the drawing are not wired up.
  • ✕No answer on the servos until you rebuild it. That needs a bench and a torque measurement.
  • ✕No exact figure for the knee pushrod ratio. We train across a range instead.
  • ✕220 hours is not a month of training. Expect repeats: one full run has already been lost.

The other nine problems we found

Thirteen faults were found in total: three reviews plus a round of my own measurements. The four at the top of this page are the ones worth deciding on. These nine are real, measured, and mostly fixed as a side effect of the same four stages.

B3

The load is backwards from a real animal

The front legs carry the least weight but do most of the pushing, and the rear legs carry the most weight but barely push. The robot is being pulled along by its front legs instead of driven by its rear.

front legs  33% of weight → 72% of push
rear legs   67% of weight → 28% of push
costThe push is coming from the legs with the least weight on them, which is exactly the condition for slipping — and follows directly from the back feet not lifting (B2).
B5

The gait is statically unstable

Sampled at twenty points around the walk cycle, the body's centre sits outside the triangle formed by the planted feet at eighteen of them.

outside the support triangle at 18 of 20 points
worst excursion  29.8 mm
costIt stays upright only because it is moving and the servos are stiff. Stop it mid-stride, or put it on an uneven surface, and there is nothing holding it up.
B6

The "three feet down at all times" guarantee is not true

The whole point of choosing a crawl was that three feet are always planted. That holds about a third of the time. The feet are bouncing, and the software is counting what it asked for rather than what happened.

three feet down  36% of the time
footfalls commanded  80 in 12 s
footfalls actually made  262
costscripts/walk.py reports the commanded count, so the number we have been reading is not a measurement. Any scoring item that asks "is this foot touching?" is currently being paid for bounce.
B7

The baseline number is one lucky draw

The project has been quoting a single run as the gait's performance. Run it six times with different random starts and the spread is enormous — and the number we quote is the best of the six on drift and below average on distance.

quoted  675.4 mm, drift 33.8 mm

costDrift is essentially random over ±360 mm. We have been comparing everything against a number that was luck, so every "better than baseline" claim to date is unsafe.
B8

The scoring harness gives wrong answers

The program that decides which saved checkpoint is best uses one random start, one commanded speed, and none of the variation that training itself uses. It has already rejected a checkpoint that is actually better.

B9

Almost all the improvement happens early. After that the score keeps creeping up because it falls over less often, not because it walks better — and the later checkpoint is measurably worse than the earlier one.

B11

The item called action_rate — "slow-changing corrections" — is meant to discourage jerky joint movement. What it is actually measuring is the deliberate random noise added during training: noise that does not exist on the real robot, where the behaviour is fixed. One penalty, not two — this is the only one that was measured.

Why the new rules do not teach the robot to stand still

Why the whole set only works because the walking prize rises

With the full proposed set, walking is worth +4.55 per control step and freezing +1.92, a gap of +2.63. Today the same figures are 4.66, 2.13 and 2.53. So the margin is preserved — slightly improved, even.

but only because
the walking prize rises from 2.0 to 3.0. Leave it at 2.0 and the identical set of changes gives a gap of 1.63 — the safety margin eroded by 36%. The two changes are not separable. Approving the new penalties without the raised prize would be approving the failure that already cost a run.
rule to carry forward
Keep the sum of all penalties at good behaviour under 0.35 × the walking prize. Today it is 0.14. This proposal puts it at 0.37 — just above its own line. That makes it the first number to watch once Stage B starts, and the first thing to trim if the robot starts looking reluctant to move.
Things we are deliberately not doing

Each of these is standard practice somewhere, and each is wrong for this machine. Listed so the plan reads as a set of choices rather than a wish list.

Rewarding time in the air (feet_air_time)

The signature reward in essentially every legged-robot project. It is designed for trotting and running, where more time in the air means a longer stride. Gray does a crawl, and a crawl wants feet down. Adding it would invert the gait we chose on purpose.

A survival bonus

Paying a small amount every step for still being upright. At the usual weight that is +6.0 for a full attempt of standing perfectly still — a quarter of the walking prize, for doing nothing. That is the exact failure that already cost us a run, rebuilt from parts. The one-shot fall penalty does the same job without being farmable.

Cost of transport as a reward

Wrong physics for a hobby servo, whose current draw is dominated by holding torque rather than motion — a stalled servo would score as free. It stays as a displayed metric, because it is interesting; it does not become an instruction.

A gait symmetry reward

Rewarding the left and right sides for moving identically. The learned layer exists partly to absorb the few-millimetre differences between legs in the CAD. A symmetry term forbids exactly the compensation we are asking for.

Posture regularisation

The usual term that pulls joints back toward a nominal standing pose. On this architecture the gait already is the nominal, so a posture term drags the whole trajectory back toward standing. It is a freeze term in disguise.

Stair climbing, and the pace gait

Not in this plan. Stairs are ruled out by the geometry (see the limits below); the pace gait is a different project.

The limits in full, with the numbers
A month of continuous training is not the right frame
everything in this plan ≈ 220 graphics-card hours
The rest of a month should be re-runs and repeat seeds — and they will be needed, because one full run has already been lost to a scoring bug that took a full run to detect. Budgeting for repeats is not pessimism; it is the observed failure rate.
The robot is currently disassembled
servo stiffness randomised 0.7–1.4× → 0.5–4.0×
The largest unquantified risk (B13) is whether the simulated servo is anywhere near the real one. It cannot be bench-measured until reassembly. The plan therefore trains across an eight-fold range of stiffness so the behaviour cannot depend on the answer. That contains the risk at no cost. It does not remove it, and it is not a substitute for measurement — it is resolved at reassembly.
No stairs
riser is taller than the hips
Not a software limitation and not fixable by training.
No closed-loop control at the joint, ever
the servos cannot report their position
Every joint command is fired blind. The robot never finds out whether a joint arrived where it was sent. This is a property of the servos, not of the code, and it bounds everything else on this page.
No absolute position or heading on the real robot
no odometry, no magnetometer
Straightness and speed can be scored in simulation, where we know exactly where the robot is. On hardware they can only be executed open-loop and then measured from outside. The robot itself has no idea where it is or which way it is pointing.
No terrain perception
camera and lidar in the CAD are not wired up
Everything here is blind walking. Nothing in this plan looks at the ground.
The knee pushrod ratio is still unmeasured
randomised 0.8–1.25×
Randomising it buys robustness across the range. It does not buy knowledge of the actual value, and should not be reported as though it did.
What the words mean
the gait — the fixed, hand-written walking pattern. It plays back like a cam profile: same motion every cycle, no reaction to anything.
the policy — the AI. The learned layer sitting on top of the gait. It does not replace the gait; it adds a small correction to each joint, capped at ±0.2 rad (about 11°).
a reward — a scoring item. Training has no goal except to make the total score as large as possible, so the rewards are the only instruction the robot ever gets. Anything not scored does not exist to it.
a round — one cycle of training: simulate for a while, then adjust. A long run is thousands of rounds.
an episode — one practice run, from standing to either falling over or running out of time.
a checkpoint — a saved copy of the learned behaviour at one round, so you can go back to it. Checkpoint 950 means "as it was at round 950".
a seed — the starting number for the simulator's random choices. Change the seed and you get a different run of the same thing. One seed is one sample, not an answer.
randomisation — deliberately varying things we are unsure of (servo lag, servo stiffness) between simulated robots, so the learned behaviour cannot depend on any one value.
the harness — the measuring method: the separate program that scores a saved copy after the fact. It is how we decide which saved copy is best. It is not the trainer.
clipped — cut down to a limit. A correction of 0.31 rad asked for against a ±0.2 rad ceiling is clipped to 0.2. The robot obeys 0.2; the trainer is never told that 0.11 of what it asked for was thrown away.
sd — standard deviation: the typical size of the wobble either side of the average. Roughly two thirds of the readings land within one sd of it.
proportional band — how far a servo has to be pushed off its commanded angle before it is producing all the torque it has. A 5.6° band means 5.6° of droop and the servo is already flat out; beyond that it just gives way.
open-loop — commanded without any feedback. Gray's servos cannot report where they actually ended up, so every joint command is fired and forgotten. Nothing in the robot ever finds out whether it arrived.
saturated — at the limit and unable to give any more. A saturated servo is already producing all the torque it has.
